What Is Image Compression?

Image compression is the process of reducing the file size of an image while keeping it looking as good as possible. There are two main types:

  • Lossy compression: Removes some data to make the file much smaller. You might lose a tiny bit of quality, but at reasonable settings most people can't tell the difference. JPEG and WebP use this.
  • Lossless compression: Reduces file size without losing any data. The file stays exactly the same quality but doesn't get as small. PNG uses this.

For most everyday uses — websites, social media, sending photos — lossy compression at 70–85% quality is the best choice. It gives you a huge reduction in file size with almost no visible difference.

Why Should You Compress Images?

  • Faster websites: Google and your visitors both hate slow sites. Compressed images load faster and improve your SEO.
  • Save phone storage: Photos can take up several megabytes each. Compressing them frees up space for more photos.
  • Easier sharing: Smaller files upload faster on WhatsApp, Telegram, email, and social media — especially on slow networks.
  • Lower data usage: If you're on a limited data plan, compressing before sending saves you money.

What Quality Setting Should You Use?

The right quality depends on what the image is for:

  • Website images: 75–85%. This gives a great balance between size and quality. Most visitors won't notice.
  • Social media: 80–90%. Platforms re-compress anyway, so starting a bit higher helps.
  • Printing: 90–100%. You need higher quality for print.
  • Thumbnails or icons: 60–70%. Small images don't need high quality.

JPG vs PNG vs WebP: Which Format Should You Use?

Choosing the right format matters just as much as the quality setting:

  • JPG (JPEG): Best for photographs and complex images. Great compression, but no transparency.
  • PNG: Best for logos, screenshots, and images that need a transparent background. Larger file sizes.
  • WebP: A modern format by Google. Better compression than JPG and PNG, and supports transparency. Supported by all major browsers now.

If you're not sure, use JPG for photos and WebP for everything on a website.
