If you're a freelancer, sending clean, professional invoices isn't optional — it's how you get paid. Yet many freelancers still send informal messages like "Hey, please pay me for that thing" and wonder why clients delay. In this guide, we'll show you exactly how to create a professional invoice, what to include, and how to use our free Invoice Generator to make one in under 60 seconds.

Why Professional Invoices Matter

  • They get you paid faster. Clear invoices remove confusion and reduce excuses for delay.
  • They look professional. Clients take you more seriously and are more likely to hire you again.
  • They protect you legally. A proper invoice is a record of work performed and payment owed.
  • They simplify your records. For tax purposes and business tracking, invoices are essential.

What Every Invoice Must Include

A professional invoice has these key elements:

  1. Your business name and contact details. Name, email, phone, and address.
  2. Client's name and contact details. Who you're billing.
  3. A unique invoice number. Something like INV-001, INV-002 — incrementing with each invoice.
  4. Invoice date. When the invoice was issued.
  5. Payment due date. When you expect to be paid (usually Net 7, Net 14, or Net 30 days).
  6. Itemized list of services. Description, quantity, and price of each item.
  7. Subtotal, taxes, and total. Clear breakdown of the amount owed.
  8. Payment instructions. Bank details, PayPal, Paystack, Wise, or whichever method you accept.

Payment Terms: What to Put on Your Invoice

Payment terms tell your client when and how to pay. Common options:

  • Due on receipt: Payment expected immediately. Best for small jobs.
  • Net 7: Payment due within 7 days. Good for freelancers who need cash flow.
  • Net 14: Payment due within 14 days. A reasonable middle ground.
  • Net 30: Payment due within 30 days. Common in corporate work but slower for you.

For new clients, use Net 7 or Net 14. For established corporate clients, Net 30 is usually standard.

Common Freelancer Invoicing Mistakes

  • No invoice number: Makes tracking and follow-up harder.
  • No due date: Some clients will delay indefinitely without a deadline.
  • Vague line items: "Design work" isn't clear. "Logo design — 3 concepts, 2 revisions" is.
  • Wrong totals: Double-check your math before sending.
  • No payment instructions: Don't make the client chase you for how to pay.
  • Waiting to send it: Send your invoice the moment the work is delivered.

How to Follow Up on Unpaid Invoices

Most clients pay on time. But when they don't, follow this sequence:

  1. Day 1 after due date: Friendly reminder email. "Just checking in — invoice #123 is now past due."
  2. Day 7: Firmer email. Reference the terms and ask for a payment date.
  3. Day 14: Phone call or video call. Direct conversation often resolves things fast.
  4. Day 30: Formal late notice. Mention any late fees agreed upon.
  5. Day 60+: Consider a collections agency or small claims court, depending on amount.

How Much Should You Charge?

Freelance rates vary by skill, industry, and location. Common models:

  • Hourly: Simplest, but caps your income at hours worked.
  • Per project: Agree a fixed price upfront. Good for well-defined work.
  • Retainer: Client pays monthly for a set amount of work. Best for stability.
  • Value-based: Charge based on the value you deliver, not hours. Highest earning potential.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need to be a registered business to invoice?

In most countries, you can invoice as an individual freelancer without a registered business. Check your local tax rules.

Can I invoice in a foreign currency?

Yes. Just be clear about the currency on the invoice (USD, GBP, EUR, etc.) and specify the payment method.

Should I charge tax on my invoices?

Depends on your country. In Nigeria, freelancers with revenue above the VAT threshold may need to charge VAT. Consult a local accountant.
